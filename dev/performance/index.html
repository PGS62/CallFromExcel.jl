Performance and alternatives

Performance

Calls are fast: a simple call takes under a millisecond, and =JuliaCall("sort", A1:A1000000) sorts a million numbers in about 0.4 seconds, including sending them to Julia and back. That's faster than Excel's own =SORT(A1:A1000000), which takes about 0.6 seconds, and about a third of the time taken by CallFromExcel's predecessor, JuliaExcel. See the changelog for more timings.

Sending data is usually the larger cost, and the one you can avoid. If many formulas use the same large range, or a result that's expensive to build, keep it in Julia as a handle: it is sent or built once, and each formula then sends only the handle's short text. Handles are cheap: 2,000 cells each creating a handle calculate in under a second (0.5 to 0.9 seconds in tests), and 2,000 JuliaCalls using them in about 0.3 seconds.

Alternatives

Apart from CallFromExcel's own predecessor, JuliaExcel, there is one other method of calling Julia from Excel of which I am aware: JuliaInXL.jl.

JuliaComputing (now JuliaHub) made JuliaInXL open source in October 2021; it previously required a licence for commercial use. When last checked (November 2026), JuliaInXL did not support dynamic array formulas or calling Julia from VBA. Filling those gaps was why I wrote JuliaExcel, this project's predecessor, in 2021.

Early versions of JuliaExcel were slower than JuliaInXL, both in latency and in moving data between Excel and Julia. That has since reversed: in tests (see workbooks/CallFromExcel-Compare.xlsm in the repository), CallFromExcel was between 1.6 and 16 times faster. For example, =jlcall("sort", A1:A1000000) takes about 4.5 seconds, and =JuliaCall("sort", A1:A1000000) about 0.4 seconds.
